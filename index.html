Intelligent System for Student Wellbeing Monitoring and Support

An AI-powered system that continuously monitors student wellbeing, detects early signs of distress, and connects students to timely, personalised support.

Learn More

Project Overview

Student Wellbeing AI is a final-year research project that applies artificial intelligence to one of the most pressing problems in higher education – the early detection and support of students experiencing psychological distress.

Current university support services are reactive: they rely on students recognising a problem and voluntarily seeking help, which usually happens only after wellbeing has seriously declined. This project proposes a proactive, integrated system that combines passive behavioural monitoring, natural-language emotion recognition, predictive risk modelling and a personalised recommendation engine into a single privacy-preserving pipeline – with a human counsellor always kept in the loop.

It is built as a React (Vite) web application with a Python (FastAPI) backend, machine-learning models served through REST APIs, and dashboards for both students and counsellors.

Literature Survey

Background

Student wellbeing has become a critical concern in higher education. Studies estimate that more than 30% of university students report symptoms of anxiety or depression, and a large proportion of these cases remain undetected because students do not self-refer to counselling services. Untreated psychological distress is strongly associated with academic underperformance, absenteeism, and student dropout. Traditional support models are reactive — they depend on a student recognising a problem and voluntarily approaching a counsellor — which means intervention often occurs only after wellbeing has significantly deteriorated.

Existing Approaches to Wellbeing Monitoring

Early research relied on periodic self-report questionnaires such as the PHQ-9, GAD-7, and WHO-5 indices. While validated, these instruments provide only a snapshot, are prone to response bias, and are completed too infrequently to capture rapidly changing emotional states. More recent work explores continuous, passive sensing using smartphone and wearable data (sleep, physical activity, screen time, location patterns) to infer mood and stress with machine-learning models. Studies such as the StudentLife project demonstrated that behavioural signals collected passively can predict changes in depression scores across an academic term.

Emotion and Sentiment Recognition

A parallel body of work applies Natural Language Processing to text that students produce naturally — journal entries, reflective writing, forum posts, and chatbot conversations. Lexicon-based methods (e.g. LIWC, VADER) were used initially, but transformer-based models such as BERT and RoBERTa now achieve substantially higher accuracy in detecting emotional distress and suicidal ideation in short informal text. Multimodal approaches that combine text with facial-expression or voice features have also been reported, though they raise stronger privacy concerns.

Predictive Risk Modelling

Several studies frame wellbeing decline as a classification or early-warning problem. Features drawn from academic records (grades, LMS logins, submission timing) combined with self-report and behavioural data have been used to train models — logistic regression, random forests, and gradient-boosting ensembles — that flag students at risk of psychological distress or academic failure with useful precision. Key challenges identified in the literature include class imbalance (few high-risk cases), concept drift across semesters, and the need for explainable predictions so that counsellors can trust and act on the output.

Personalised Support and Intervention

Digital mental-health interventions — including CBT-based mobile apps, mindfulness tools, and conversational agents such as Woebot and Wysa — have shown small-to-moderate positive effects on student anxiety and depression in controlled trials. Recommender-system techniques are increasingly used to match students to the most appropriate resource (peer support, self-help content, or professional referral) based on their current state.

Research Gap

Although individual components — passive sensing, sentiment analysis, risk prediction, and digital intervention — are well studied in isolation, few systems integrate them into a single continuous pipeline that monitors wellbeing, explains its assessment, and routes the student to timely, personalised support while preserving privacy and keeping a human counsellor in the loop. This project addresses that gap by developing an integrated intelligent system for student wellbeing monitoring and support.

System Components

Component 1 – Behavioural & Mood Monitoring

Collects lightweight daily check-ins (mood, sleep, stress rating) and optional passive signals (activity level, app-usage patterns, LMS login frequency). Aggregates these into a per-student wellbeing timeline, highlighting sudden drops and sustained negative patterns, and exposes privacy-preserving early-warning signals to the counselling team.

Component 2 – Emotion & Sentiment Recognition (NLP)

Analyses free-text that students produce naturally – journal entries, reflective notes and chatbot messages – using Google’s Generative AI (Gemini) to classify emotional state and detect high-risk language such as hopelessness or self-harm indicators, complementing the numeric wellbeing indicators.

Component 3 – Wellbeing Risk Prediction

Combines features from Components 1 and 2 with academic-engagement data to produce an early-warning risk score (low / moderate / high) using a gradient-boosting model (XGBoost / LightGBM), with explainable output (SHAP) so counsellors understand why a student was flagged.

Component 4 – Monitoring, Visualization & AI Learning Support Dashboard

A conversational agent plus a recommender that matches each student to the most appropriate resource: self-help content, guided exercises (CBT / mindfulness), peer support, or a direct referral to a counsellor. High-risk cases trigger an alert to the wellbeing team.

Research Problem & Solution

Proposed Problem

Existing student-support models are reactive, fragmented, and depend on self-referral. Individual AI techniques – passive sensing, sentiment analysis, risk prediction and digital intervention – are well studied in isolation but are rarely integrated into one continuous, explainable, privacy-preserving pipeline that keeps a human counsellor in control.

Proposed Solution

An integrated intelligent system that continuously monitors wellbeing indicators, recognises emotional distress from natural language, predicts risk of decline with explainable models, and delivers timely personalised support – while keeping a human counsellor in control of all high-risk interventions. Data flows from the student's check-ins and journal entries into Components 1 and 2, whose outputs feed the risk-prediction model (Component 3), which in turn drives the recommendation and conversational-support module (Component 4), while a counsellor dashboard surfaces early-warning signals. Main Objective

“To design and develop an intelligent system for continuous monitoring, assessment, and support of student wellbeing and mental health, enabling earlier identification of at-risk students and timely, personalised intervention.”

Objective 1 – Design and develop a system to collect, store and visualise multi-source student wellbeing indicators (mood, behaviour, academic engagement)

Design a short, low-friction daily check-in (mood, sleep, stress rating)

Capture optional passive signals: activity level, app-usage patterns, LMS login frequency

Store and aggregate the data into a per-student wellbeing timeline

Visualise trends for students and privacy-preserving early-warning signals for counsellors

Objective 2 – Develop an NLP model to recognise emotion and detect high-risk language in student-generated text

Collect and anonymise consented text: journal entries, reflective notes, chatbot messages

Integrate Google Generative AI (Gemini) for emotion and sentiment classification

Detect high-risk language such as hopelessness or self-harm indicators

Surface language-based wellbeing insights alongside the numeric indicators

Objective 3 – Develop an explainable predictive model that identifies students at risk of declining wellbeing early enough for effective intervention

Engineer features from Components 1 and 2 plus LMS engagement and grades

Train and evaluate gradient-boosting models (XGBoost, LightGBM); handle class imbalance (SMOTE)

Produce a low / moderate / high risk score with SHAP explanations

Provide a consent-based alert to the counselling team for students at elevated risk

Objective 4 – Design and evaluate a personalised recommendation and conversational-support module that routes students to appropriate resources

Build a rule- and content-based recommender over a resource catalogue

Add an intent-based conversational agent (Rasa / LLM) for first-line support

Match students to self-help content, guided exercises, peer support or a counsellor referral

Escalate high-risk cases with an alert to the wellbeing team

Objective 5 – Evaluate the integrated system for accuracy, usability and ethical / privacy compliance

Measure model accuracy and the quality of end-to-end risk detection

Run usability testing with students and counsellors on both dashboards

Review data handling against ethical approval and privacy requirements

Refine the system based on evaluation findings

Methodology

A requirements study was carried out using online questionnaires and interviews with university students and counselling staff. Insights from this study informed the design of the four components. The system follows a client-server architecture: a React (Vite) front end for students and counsellors, a Python (FastAPI) back end exposing each component as a REST service, and a database for check-in history, journal entries and model data. The emotion/sentiment and wellbeing-risk models are trained offline and served through the back end; the recommendation module consumes their outputs at runtime.

Overall process: requirement analysis → dataset collection & ethical approval → model development & training → API integration → web application development → integrated testing → user evaluation with students and counsellors → refinement.
